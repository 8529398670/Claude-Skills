# The four levels

Read the one you picked. These are different documents for different people, not the same
document at four vocabulary settings — the *content* changes, not just the wording.

A quick way to check you got it right: if two levels of the same topic would share a
paragraph, at least one of them is wrong.

---

## eli5

A curious person with no background in the field. Simple, concrete language — but **never**
condescending, never baby talk, and never "imagine you're a tiny particle!" unless that
genuinely helps.

- No equations. Numbers only as concrete counts and comparisons: "twice as far", "about as
  heavy as a car".
- Analogies to everyday physical experience, each flagged as an analogy, with one line on
  where it breaks. An unflagged analogy becomes a false belief.
- **Foreground the single most important lever and let the others go.** Breadth is the
  enemy here. One thing understood beats four things half-recognised.
- Widgets are big, tactile, immediate, with obvious cause and effect. Cartoon clarity beats
  precision.
- ~600–1000 words of prose. 3–4 widgets.

This band is tighter than it looks, and it is the one most often overrun. The scarce
resource is a child's attention, not the page — so when you are over, the fix is almost
never shorter sentences. It is one fewer thing you tried to say.

The trap: writing a college document in small words. If your explanation still depends on
the reader accepting something on faith, find the version that does not.

---

## high_school

Has algebra and basic science vocabulary. Ready for real terminology if you introduce it.

- Simple equations are welcome and should be shown. Define every symbol at first use.
- Introduce the field's actual terms alongside plain language, so the reader can look things
  up afterwards. Leaving someone unable to search for more is a real cost.
- 2–3 mechanisms.
- **This is where labelled graphs belong.** Let the reader move a parameter and watch a
  curve change shape — the jump from "formula" to "shape of a relationship" is the main
  thing this level is for.
- ~900–1400 words. 4–5 widgets.

---

## college

Has calculus and has taken an introductory course in the parent field.

- The actual governing relationships, named properly, with the real equations. Sketch a
  derivation where it illuminates rather than decorates.
- State assumptions. Units matter. Orders of magnitude matter.
- 3–5 mechanisms, including second-order effects the simpler levels ignored.
- Widgets should sweep parameters **across regimes**, so the reader sees a qualitative
  change — a transition, a threshold, a limiting case — not just a number moving. Someone
  at this level has already seen numbers move.
- ~1200–2000 words. 4–6 widgets.

---

## expert

Works in or adjacent to this field. Assume everything.

- **Skip all motivation and all definitions.** No "recall that". No history. If you find
  yourself explaining what the topic is, you have the wrong level.
- Go straight to what is actually interesting: where the standard treatment is wrong or
  incomplete, competing formulations and the grounds for choosing between them, the failure
  modes practitioners actually hit, what the literature disagrees about, what is open.
- Full formalism, unapologetically.
- Widgets explore regimes that matter for real decisions, compare competing models side by
  side, or expose edge cases where the textbook answer misleads.
- ~1200–2000 words. 4–6 widgets.

**This must not read as a longer college document.** Different content, not more content.
If an expert would already know a sentence you wrote, cut it — and if that leaves the page
thin, you have not yet found the thing worth saying to this reader.

The honesty rules bite hardest here. An expert will recognise a fabricated citation
immediately, and it costs you the whole document.
